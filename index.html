Accounts to get

The lab manager will provision most of these before your start date. If anything is missing, message them on Discord.

  • University email — your primary address for all official communication. VPN access comes with it.
  • GitHub — accept the invitation to github.com/[your-org] (sent to your university email).
  • Discord — primary day-to-day lab communication. Invite sent by the lab manager.
  • HPC cluster — request via the IT ticket system; CC the PI. Usually takes 2–3 business days.
  • Google Drive — shared lab folder for protocols, meeting notes, and manuscripts. Added by the lab manager.
  • IRB training — required before any human-subjects data collection. Complete via the university's compliance portal.

Email

Email is for external collaborators, formal communications, and anything that needs a paper trail.

When to use email vs. Discord

  • Use email for: contacting people outside the lab, submitting IRB amendments, communicating with journal editors, anything that needs CC or BCC.
  • Use Discord for: everything else within the lab — questions, updates, sharing papers, scheduling.

Response expectations

  • Reply to lab emails within one working day.
  • If the PI emails you, same expectation — but a brief acknowledgment ("Got it, I'll look at this by Friday") counts.
  • Archive as you go. A well-labelled inbox saves you later.
Tip: Set up email signature with your name, role, lab name, and pronouns if you share them. It makes it easier for collaborators to reply correctly.

Discord

Discord is the lab's hub for day-to-day communication. It's async-first — no expectation of instant replies.

Key channels

  • #introductions — post a short intro when you join (name, background, what you'll be working on).
  • #general — lab-wide announcements and conversation.
  • #papers — share papers worth reading; add a one-line summary.
  • #computing-help — HPC, R, and GitHub questions. No question is too basic.
  • #random — everything else.
  • Project channels — each active project has its own channel; the lab manager adds you to yours.

Norms

  • A reply within one working day is the norm. Turn off notifications in the evening if you need to — there is no expectation you're online after hours.
  • Use threads for extended conversations so channels stay readable.
  • Add an emoji reaction (👍) to confirm you've seen a message, rather than a "got it" reply that pings everyone.
  • Direct messages are fine for 1:1 questions, but prefer public channels for things others might benefit from.
Tip: Set Discord to "Do not disturb" on your calendar's focus blocks. The lab respects async boundaries.

Meetings

A regular meeting cadence keeps everyone aligned without over-scheduling.

  • Lab meeting — Fridays 10–11:30 am. Each member presents 2–3× per year. Add agenda items to the shared doc by Thursday evening.
  • Project check-ins — weekly or biweekly with your direct supervisor. 20–30 min; you set the agenda.
  • One-on-one with PI — monthly for postdocs, biweekly for first- and second-year grad students.
  • Journal club — Wednesdays 12–1 pm. Alternates between domain-specific and methods papers. Sign up on the shared sheet.
Whoever runs a meeting takes notes. Notes go in the shared Drive under Meetings/[YYYY-MM-DD].

Lab norms

These aren't rules so much as the working agreements that let the lab function well.

  • Open science by default. Pre-register studies, share data and analysis code on acceptance. If there's a reason not to, talk to the PI first.
  • Authorship is discussed early. Before a project is a month old, have an explicit conversation about contributions and expected authorship order. Revisit it when the scope changes.
  • Null results belong in the lab. Share what didn't work internally — it saves others from repeating dead ends.
  • Feedback is a gift. Be direct and specific. If feedback felt unfair, say so — that's information too.
  • Credit generously. Name collaborators in talks and in your code's acknowledgements. It costs nothing and builds trust.
  • Ask before you assume. If you're unsure about scope, timelines, or expectations, ask in #general or directly in Discord.

Lab philosophy

Five interlocking pillars define how we work, mentor, and grow together. These aren't abstract ideals — they shape how we run meetings, give feedback, assign authorship, and support each other day to day.

1

Belong to be challenged

A psychologically safe environment where honest feedback flows without fear.

2

Reciprocal expectations

Mentorship as a documented, mutual agreement — explicit in both directions.

3

Structured skill development

Systematic training in research, communication, and leadership across career stages.

4

Rigorous, ethical research practice

Responsible conduct embedded in routine lab life, not treated as a one-off training.

5

Continuous reflection & feedback

A growth mindset for mentor and mentees alike — evolving through honest dialogue.

Belong to be challenged

See also: Code of conduct

The leader's first responsibility is to cultivate a psychologically healthy research environment where mentees can give, receive, and act on feedback without fear of personal attack, retaliation, or humiliation. This includes explicitly normalizing that everyone makes mistakes, treating lapses as opportunities to learn, and addressing uncivil or insensitive behavior through calm, non-defensive conversations that focus on patterns rather than isolated incidents.

A humanistic approach emphasizes respect and compassion for every lab member regardless of career stage, background, or role, recognizing that people bring diverse histories and tolerance levels for incivility to work. The mentor sets the tone by modeling dignity and respect in all interactions and by ensuring a safe and supportive environment free from harassment and discrimination, consistent with institutional policies and mandatory reporting obligations.

Diverse perspectives make better science

Science is enriched — not just ethically, but intellectually — by people who think differently. Lab members come to this work with different cultural backgrounds, educational histories, career trajectories, and ways of framing problems. Those differences are a resource. A question that seems naive from one vantage point may expose an assumption the rest of the group has been silently carrying for months. An unconventional interpretation of a dataset, a method borrowed from an adjacent field, an instinct shaped by a different kind of training — these are the edges where discovery tends to happen.

We are therefore intentional about making space for a full range of voices in lab meetings, paper discussions, and experimental design. This means pushing back on the tendency to defer to seniority or confidence, and actively inviting perspectives from people who may be less likely to volunteer them. No one should feel that their background makes their contributions less credible here.

Giving and receiving critique

Critical feedback is one of the most valuable things we give one another — and one of the hardest to do well. The goal of critique in this lab is not to identify flaws for their own sake, but to strengthen the work and develop the scientist. That distinction shapes how we give feedback and how we receive it.

When giving feedback

  • →Be specific. Vague praise or vague criticism are both hard to act on. Name the sentence, the analysis step, the logical gap.
  • →Critique the work, not the person. "This conclusion isn't supported by the data shown" lands differently than "you misread the data."
  • →Pair problems with possibilities. If you flag an issue, try to suggest at least one direction forward — even if that direction is "I'm not sure, but it's worth exploring."
  • →Consider the moment. A lab meeting mid-presentation and a one-on-one manuscript review call for different registers. Read the context.

When receiving feedback

  • →Try to hear the critique before responding to it. Defensiveness is a natural first reflex — let it pass before deciding how to reply.
  • →Ask for specifics if you don't have them. "Can you say more about what you mean?" is always a reasonable response.
  • →You don't have to agree. Feedback is data, not instruction. Consider it seriously, then decide — with your own judgment — what to do with it.
  • →Close the loop. When you've acted on feedback, say so. It signals that the exchange was worthwhile and encourages the reviewer to keep engaging.
A note on discomfort: Being challenged — having a hypothesis questioned, a method pushed back on, a draft returned with significant revisions — can feel discouraging. That discomfort is not a sign that something has gone wrong. It is usually a sign that something is about to get better. The lab's job is to make sure that challenge is delivered with care, and your job is to meet it with openness.

Reciprocal expectations

Mentorship as a mutual agreement — explicit, documented, and revisited over time.

Effective mentorship is framed as a mutual agreement with clearly articulated expectations in both directions, ideally documented in a living "lab expectations" or advisor–advisee agreement co-revised over time. The mentor specifies expectations around honesty, communication, lab presence, participation in lab community, authorship discussion, and contribution to shared lab responsibilities — while also stating what trainees can reliably expect from the mentor: regular meetings, timely feedback, advocacy, networking support, letters of recommendation, and help planning timelines and funding.

Mentees are responsible for their own careers: actively seeking advice, critically evaluating it, and taking ownership of scientific direction within the broad themes of the lab — including raising concerns, setting boundaries, and considering lab changes if interests diverge. Regular one-on-one check-ins and explicit conversations about meeting frequency, workload, and perceived inequities help prevent isolation, misaligned expectations, and resentment.

In practice: This is why the expectations section of this guide is bidirectional — what the PI expects from you, and what you can expect from the PI. Both are commitments.

Structured skill development

Mentorship extends well beyond data and manuscripts.

Mentorship encompasses systematic development of research, communication, and leadership skills across career stages. This means intentional training in study design, power analysis, lab notebook practice, sample management, solution preparation, and data management — using SOPs and templates to scaffold good habits and reproducible work.

Professional development includes guiding course choices, conference participation, grant writing, and outreach, and encouraging mentees to pursue independent ideas that align with lab themes while learning to "sell" those ideas through proposals and presentations.

A core expectation is that more senior mentees practice mentoring themselves — for example, by supervising undergraduates — with the PI supporting this as both a training experience and a way to propagate healthy mentoring practices through the group.

Rigorous, ethical research practice

Responsible conduct of research is woven into routine lab life, not saved for a one-time training.

This includes explicit instruction and ongoing discussion about scientific rigor: reproducibility, accurate record-keeping, appropriate statistics, data normalization, and outlier handling. It also covers authorship norms, avoidance of gift or ghost authorship, and appropriate credit through authorship or acknowledgments based on material and intellectual contribution.

All lab members are expected to complete required RCR and safety trainings, understand ethical and regulatory expectations (human and animal subjects, biosafety, data confidentiality, conflicts of interest), and follow clear policies for data ownership, sharing, and handling during lab transitions and departures.

Data ownership: The PI is custodian of all lab data. Raw data, analysis scripts, and lab notebooks remain with the lab when a member departs. Talk to the PI before your end date about archiving your work in the shared lab repositories.

Continuous reflection & growth

A growth mindset applies to both mentor and mentees.

A central tenet is ongoing reflection and willingness to seek advice, adapt, and improve communication and leadership skills. The mentor regularly invites candid feedback about the lab climate and their own mentoring, treats criticism as valuable data, and — when missteps occur — de-escalates, acknowledges impact, and works with mentees to decide how to move forward constructively.

Mentees are encouraged to self-assess strengths and areas for development, to signal when they need more structure or support, and to build a broader mentoring network beyond the primary PI.

Throughout, mentorship is a collaborative, evolving relationship in which mentor and mentees co-create a respectful, rigorous, and inclusive research community — one that supports both excellent science and sustainable, humane careers.

Expectations by role

Each role carries different responsibilities and comes with different support structures. Full bidirectional expectations are detailed in the expectations sections below.

Undergraduate students

Volunteer
  • Undergrad volunteers are a core part of the lab community and treated as full members.
  • Expectations are calibrated to your time commitment and experience level — this is a learning environment.
  • You will be paired with a graduate student or postdoc mentor for day-to-day guidance.
  • Strong performers are encouraged to pursue independent projects, present at lab meeting, and apply for undergraduate research awards.

Graduate students

M.S.
Ph.D.
  • Master's students typically focus on one well-scoped project with clear deliverables. The PI works closely with you on experimental design and writing.
  • Doctoral students are expected to develop increasing independence over the course of the program — from co-designed projects in years 1–2 toward self-directed research by years 4–5.
  • Both are expected to attend and contribute to lab meetings, pursue funding, mentor junior members, and engage with the broader Hatfield community.
  • Graduate program requirements (coursework, committee meetings, qualifying exams) are your responsibility to track — but the PI will help you plan.
GRA note: GRAs are funded for approximately 20 hours of research per week; the other 20 hours are allocated to coursework. Plan your workload accordingly and talk to the PI early if you feel these are in conflict.

Faculty Research Assistants

FRA
  • FRAs are the operational backbone of the lab — responsible for keeping life support systems, instrumentation, and shared spaces in working order across multiple projects.
  • Your responsibilities span a wide range: benchwork, instrumental chemistry, organism husbandry, field research, shop fabrication, and facilities.
  • Close communication with the PI is essential to stay aligned as projects evolve and priorities shift.
  • FRAs do not receive overtime pay — if workload feels unsustainable, raise it early.

Postdoctoral fellows

Postdoc
  • Postdocs are expected to lead the projects agreed upon at hiring, while recognizing that scientific plans evolve.
  • You are encouraged to develop independent ideas that align with lab themes and to actively pursue your own funding — fellowship applications and independent grants are a central part of postdoctoral training.
  • Senior postdocs are expected to co-mentor graduate students and to contribute to lab culture and intellectual life.
  • Career development conversations happen regularly in 1:1s — the PI is invested in what comes next for you, whether that's faculty positions, research scientist roles, or beyond academia.

Code of conduct

This guide is iterative — input from colleagues and lab members is expected to strengthen the lab mission. Feedback is welcome at samuel.gurr@oregonstate.edu

Belonging

We respect and value people from all walks of life. Diverse perspectives drive knowledge and creativity, and learning from peers and colleagues helps us become better citizens and scientists.

Belonging is a fundamental human need. In the GurrLab, we foster it through the availability of our group, individuals, places, times, and spaces — weekly team meetings, individual check-ins, and lab-wide social events all play a role.

As a member of the GurrLab, all walks of life are accepted. Discrimination is never acceptable.

Psychological safety

A psychologically safe and harassment-free working environment is pivotal to enable us to belong and be challenged together.

"Psychological safety is defined as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. Such interpersonal risks include open communication, voicing concerns, asking questions, and seeking feedback without fear of judgment. A psychologically safe environment allows all individuals to be their authentic selves and reduce work-related stress, leading to increased engagement and learning."

Citation: Hardie, P., O'Donovan, R., Jarvis, S., & Redmond, C. (2022). Key tips to providing a psychologically safe learning environment in the clinical setting. BMC Medical Education, 22(1), 816.

Harassment

Harassment includes offensive verbal comments related to gender, gender identity and expression, age, sexual orientation, disability, socioeconomic status, physical appearance, body size, race, religion (or lack thereof), sexual images in public spaces, deliberate intimidation, stalking, following, harassing photography or recording, sustained disruption of talks or other events, inappropriate physical contact, and unwelcome sexual attention.

Members asked to stop any harassing behavior are expected to comply immediately.

Mental health

Your overall well-being matters, both as individuals and as members of this lab. A safe, respectful, and supportive working environment is essential for good science and for sustaining a healthy team.

I want to acknowledge my role and its limits. While I am committed to being an advocate for you and to fostering a positive lab culture, I am not a trained mental health professional. If you are dealing with mental health challenges, stress, or personal difficulties, I strongly encourage you to seek support from qualified professionals.

As your mentor, I am willing to listen, help connect you with resources, and work with you on reasonable accommodations within the scope of the lab.

Counseling & Psychological Services (CAPS) at OSU — confidential support for students.

What I expect from you

These expectations apply to all lab members, with role-specific notes where relevant. They are meant to support your growth, not to micromanage it.

Your career is yours to drive

I expect that you will seek advice on your professional and academic development — including classes, teaching, research directions, funding opportunities, conferences, and job applications — and that you will seriously consider that advice. At the same time, I expect you to use your own judgment and make decisions independently. You are in charge of your path.

Graduate and undergraduate students: I expect that your research will have meaningful overlap with my expertise and interests, so that I can mentor you effectively. It is also helpful to stay aware of your peers' projects and their funding sources. If your interests shift significantly from the lab's themes, please bring that to me early so we can discuss options — including pivoting direction or transitioning to another lab. Keeping that conversation open is healthy, not a failure.

Faculty Research Assistants: I expect that you joined the laboratory knowledgeable of its current status, research goals, members, equipment, and partnerships. Your main responsibility is to keep the lab in working order across a wide range of tasks — benchwork, instrumental chemistry, husbandry, field research, shop/builds, electrical, and plumbing. Close communication keeps everything running.

Postdoctoral fellows: I expect that you will pursue the experiments we agreed on when you joined as your main project, while recognizing that plans will evolve as the science develops. I am always happy to discuss new directions, and when my funding is supporting the work, I expect to be closely involved in those decisions.

Senior Research Associates: In addition to postdoctoral expectations, I expect that you will strive to secure your own research support.

Communication keeps the machine running

  • Be honest. You're here to learn and ask questions, and everyone makes mistakes. If something goes astray, please let me know. This is a judgment-free zone — we learn from our mistakes — but I need to know when things come up to ensure the academic integrity and safety of the lab.
  • Respect your colleagues, mentors, and mentees. A sense of belonging starts with how we treat one another. Expect to both give and receive productive critique.
  • Be resourceful. I don't know all the answers, and you will often learn more effectively by figuring things out yourself. Ask questions freely — sometimes I'll have an answer, and sometimes I'll point you toward useful paths.
  • Respect each other's time. If we have a meeting scheduled, please be on time or give me a heads-up if you'll be late. I will do the same.
  • Be a full participant in the lab community. Attend lab meetings on time, contribute actively (leading themes, presenting, asking questions), join social events, and take part in outreach. Be supportive of your labmates — say hello, help when asked, and welcome newcomers.
  • Tell me when you can't do something. You are juggling as many things as I am, and sometimes you'll need to say no. That's fine — I don't expect you to do everything.
  • Respond to emails within 24–48 hours during the work week. Let me know if you'll be out of contact for an extended period.
  • Alert the team when something is wrong in the lab. Review the communication lines listed on this site and make the most reasonable decision for how to inform the team or me.

Work hours

I generally expect your presence in the lab about five days a week, averaging 8–10 hours per day. I tend to work late, but I do not expect you to follow my schedule or answer emails after standard work hours. I will not monitor your exact arrival and departure times, but I do expect steady, consistent progress reflecting a standard work week — taking into account teaching and coursework obligations.

Atypical hours can be considered if the project permits, though I strongly encourage daytime hours: they help sustain participation in the lab community and make it easier to get assistance from staff and peers.

Note for graduate students: GRAs are typically funded for 20 hours of research per week, with the remaining time allocated to coursework. Plan accordingly.

Ownership over your research

  • Prioritize life support of your organisms. Your experimental systems and husbandry schedule deserve diligence and attention to prevent unplanned losses. Hatfield has a resident veterinarian who conducts unannounced checks. Neglect of organisms in any shared space will not be tolerated.
  • Complete lab responsibilities on time. The lab functions effectively only when everyone contributes reliably. This includes maintaining cleanliness and being considerate in shared spaces, with instrumentation, power tools, and supplies. If there isn't enough time to clean up properly, there isn't enough time to run the experiment.
  • Send a summary within 24 hours of our 1:1 meetings. Brief bullets covering discussion points and action items are enough. This keeps us accountable and avoids redundancy.
  • Discuss authorship early. Talk with me before submitting or presenting work. PIs typically contribute intellectually and financially and are listed as senior authors. If a side project did not involve my effort in those ways, I would not expect authorship — and doing side projects is great, as long as it doesn't pull you from your core responsibilities.
  • Actively pursue funding. Learning to sell your ideas is one of the most important parts of your training. Grant success will be a major consideration for future employers in research careers. I will help fund your research, but I need to see that you are actively working to support yourself.
  • Mentor an undergraduate or high school student at least once during your time in the lab. It's an opportunity to give back, build your own mentoring skills, and reflect on whether advising students is part of the career you want. Talk to me if you'd like to recruit someone — I often know of students who have already expressed interest.

What you can expect from me

My commitments to you as your mentor and PI.

  • Regular meetings. You can expect to meet with me for at least 30 minutes every 1–2 weeks. I will be on time and will let you know if I'm running late. Drop-ins and brief questions are welcome — if my door is open, feel free to ask.
  • Honest feedback. I will regularly give you candid feedback on your progress and regularly ask for yours on the lab, your experience, and my mentorship. I can't improve what I'm not aware of, and I am committed to acting on your input.
  • Clear deadlines and expectations. I will help you set reasonable timelines for progress and define what that progress should look like — what we want and when we want it.
  • Timely feedback on writing. I will return feedback on grants and papers within 1–2 weeks, unless the material is unusually complex. I will also try to respond to emails within 24 hours during the work week. If you don't hear from me, send a reminder and ping me on Discord.
  • Advocacy. I will listen to your concerns and help you solve problems. When a situation needs to be escalated, I will step in and advocate for you — with the department, the graduate program, collaborators, or wherever is needed.
  • Letters of recommendation. I will write letters for you with reasonable notice. For a first letter or a new type of letter, please give me two weeks — I need a solid block of time to write something strong. After that, one week of lead time is sufficient. I understand that opportunities sometimes arise unexpectedly and will do my best in a rush, but please don't count on that as the norm.
  • Networking. I will introduce you to people in the field, connect you at conferences, and help you understand what to expect from those interactions — having a sharp elevator pitch and knowing what to ask matters more than most people realize.
  • Investment in your talks and posters. These are your face to the research community. If the audience is giving you their time, I want to help you make the most of it. I will give you my time to make your presentations effective and engaging.
  • Timeline oversight. I will keep a rough eye on where you are relative to program milestones and help you plan so there are no surprises. Ultimately, you are responsible for hitting deadlines — but I'll help you see them coming.
  • Funding opportunities. I will share funding sources — research grants, fellowships, teaching opportunities — and help you decide what to pursue.
  • Effort matched to yours. I will work as hard toward your success as you do. The harder you push, the harder I will push to support you.
  • A safe working environment. I will ensure the lab is a safe and supportive place to work. If you have concerns about the lab climate, I want to know immediately. As a faculty member, I am a mandatory reporter — I must report instances of harassment or abuse — but all information remains confidential unless you choose to release it.
  • Professional modeling. I will provide a model of professionalism in academia and can offer guidance on conflicts of interest, equity and fairness, ethical research, and mentoring.
  • Additional mentors and resources. I will help you build a broader mentoring network. No advisor can provide everything you'll need across a career — developing multiple mentoring relationships early is one of the best investments you can make.
